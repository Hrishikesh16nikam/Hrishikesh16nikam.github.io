Closing the evidence gap: how a security brain turns “a risk exists” into “here’s everyone it touches”

Hrishikesh Nikam MS Computer Engineering (Networks & Security), Northeastern October 2026

Sola’s own research found that frontier AI models already reason well about security. What they lack is the map of how an organization’s systems connect. This case study walks through what Sola is building to supply that map, works through one realistic identity scenario end to end, and proposes three product ideas aimed at the gap the research measured.

Written from Sola’s public website, research and press coverage, as of October 2026. I haven’t used the product, so some of these ideas may already exist or be on the roadmap.

In short

  • Sola’s Security Brain is a context layer: a continuously updated graph that unifies cloud, identity, SaaS, endpoint and code systems so people and agents get one evidence-backed answer.
  • In Sola’s benchmark, the best setup reached the right verdict 94% of the time but a complete, evidence-backed answer 78% of the time. That 16-point gap is where remediation breaks.
  • I propose three ideas aimed at that gap: show evidence coverage on every signal, let analysts confirm identity links, and turn severity overrides into reusable business context.

What Sola is building

Security teams run dozens of tools, and Sola puts the average at upwards of 45. Each tool sees one slice of the environment, while the context that decides what matters (policies, playbooks, which assets are production) sits in documents and people’s heads. Sola’s answer is to sit between the raw data and the people or agents asking questions.

Security Brain

A graph that precomputes relationships, blast radius, ownership and business context across the whole estate. It abstracts vendor vocabulary (an AWS “IAM Role,” an Azure “Principal”) into one model, guides the AI with expert-written queries, and validates every generated query before it runs.

Lumina Signals

A daily feed of pre-investigated risk signals. Each one carries severity that can move up or down with business context, clustered related findings, blast radius, glass-box reasoning and a remediation action to approve.

Research

Published benchmarks on how AI agents perform at identity security, plus Open.Security, which tests openly how agents reason and fail in realistic enterprise conditions.

Two design choices stand out to me. First, the layer is read-only and governed, replacing homegrown agents that each hold their own production credentials. Second, Sola builds understanding once and keeps it current, so a question becomes a lookup rather than a fresh, expensive exploration.

The insight that matters most: context beats model choice

Sola’s cross-platform identity benchmark tested four frontier models on 50 tasks spanning eight systems, including HiBob, Okta, Azure AD, AWS, GCP, Google Workspace, GitHub and MongoDB Atlas. Each model ran with five levels of supporting context, from raw access to schemas plus a cross-vendor relationship map plus example queries.

34%relative lift in answer correctness from no context to full context
~70%fewer exploratory queries per task with full context
94% vs 78%right verdict vs complete, evidence-backed answer (best model, full context)

The models reasoned well even without help, scoring 0.94 to 0.99 on reasoning utility. They failed at the evidence layer: querying tables that didn’t exist, assuming wrong links between systems, and missing affected identities even after correctly spotting the risk. The research calls the underlying problem the correlation gap, which means linking the same identity across systems that share no common key.

Knowing a privilege escalation path exists doesn’t close it. You need the full list of identities it touches.

That’s why I think the 94-versus-78 gap is the most important number on the site. It’s the distance between a good alert and a safe remediation, and it’s exactly what a context layer is for.

A worked scenario: the contractor who was offboarded everywhere but one place

To make the correlation gap concrete, here is an illustrative scenario. It’s synthetic and not based on Sola output, but it’s built from the kinds of cross-system risks Sola’s research names: offboarded employees who keep access, and GitHub members with no matching identity in the identity provider.

A contractor, J. Rivera, finishes on October 3. HR marks the record as terminated and the Okta account is suspended the same day. But Rivera joined the company’s GitHub organization with a personal account, jr-codes, before SSO was enforced. That account still has write access to the deployment repository, whose CI pipeline assumes a production AWS role.

An agent with raw access only

Finds the terminated HR record and the suspended Okta account, and concludes offboarding is complete. The verdict is directionally reasonable and the evidence is incomplete, which is the failure mode the benchmark measured.

An agent grounded in a relationship map

  • Links jr-codes to the HR record through commit emails and name history.
  • Follows its permissions to the deploy repo and the production role.
  • Returns one finding with the full chain as evidence.

Here’s how that finding could look as a decision-ready signal, following the structure Sola describes for Lumina:

Writing this out showed me where the hard parts are. The signal is only as trustworthy as the identity link, and an analyst can only act confidently if they know the evidence is complete. My three ideas come from those two observations.

Three ideas aimed at the evidence gap

01

Show evidence coverage on every signal

The benchmark’s core finding is that a correct verdict can still come with an incomplete list of affected identities. An analyst can’t see that from the verdict alone. A short coverage line would make completeness visible instead of assumed.

Sources checkedHiBob, Okta, GitHub, AWS
Not checkedGitLab (connector disconnected 3 days ago)
Affected identities1, from a deterministic graph query
Data freshnessAll sources synced in the last 2 hours
Why it helps

It tells the analyst exactly when to trust a signal and when to dig further, and it separates counts that came from deterministic queries from anything the model enumerated itself.

How I’d measure it

Track how often analysts re-investigate a signal before acting, with and without the coverage line.

02

Let analysts confirm or reject identity links

Bridging an Okta email to an AWS role or a GitHub login is inference, and some inferences will be wrong. Showing each inferred link with its basis, plus a confirm or reject control, turns every analyst decision into ground truth for that customer’s graph.

Why it helps

The correlation gap shrinks over time for each customer, and signals that depend on unconfirmed links can be marked as such, which matters most for automated remediation.

How I’d measure it

The share of signals that rely on unconfirmed links, and the rejection rate of proposed links, both trending down week over week.

03

Turn severity overrides into reusable business context

Lumina’s severity already moves with business context. When an analyst still disagrees and moves a score, that disagreement is valuable data. A one-tap reason (“staging asset,” “break-glass account,” “owned by a vendor”) could become a proposed calibration rule, previewed before it applies: “This would lower 14 open signals that match *-stg-*.”

Why it helps

Each correction is made once instead of every day, and the reasoning stays inspectable, which fits Sola’s glass-box approach and gives GRC teams an audit trail for prioritization.

How I’d measure it

Manual override rate per week, which should fall as calibration rules accumulate.

Questions I’d ask the team

  1. When the graph can’t link two identities with confidence, how is that uncertainty surfaced to agents that query the brain, rather than to people?
  2. How do you keep the precomputed graph consistent when a connector lags or fails, and does a signal get invalidated when its underlying evidence changes?
  3. Which parts of a Lumina investigation are deterministic queries and which are model reasoning, and how did you decide where that line sits?

Why this problem interests me

I’ve been working on the same tension between AI judgment and deterministic guarantees from a few directions:

  • Software development co-op at Cybersenz: built AI agents for voice and fax intake in a privacy-sensitive setting.
  • Software Developer Intern at IBM: working on AI-driven compliance workflows.
  • Triage Inbox: a project where the AI may only choose from fixed labels, identifiers are redacted before any model sees them, and every action lands in an HMAC-chained audit log that detects tampering.
  • L4/L7 load balancer and CRDT collaborative editor: systems work on health checks, failover and keeping distributed state consistent.
  • MS in Computer Engineering with a Networks & Security concentration at Northeastern, graduating December 2026.

A small experiment I’d like to run next: a synthetic HR, identity provider, GitHub and AWS dataset with a few hundred identities, comparing an agent given only schemas against one given a relationship map, and scoring evidence completeness rather than verdicts. It’s a miniature version of the question Sola’s research asks, and I’d learn a lot from seeing the gap first-hand.
